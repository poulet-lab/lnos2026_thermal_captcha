# Thermal Captcha as a Thermo-Tactile Psychophysics Opportunity

## Initial assessment

The current **Thermal Captcha** task is already more than a booth gimmick: it is a cross-modal identification paradigm in which participants feel a brief thermal waveform and then choose the visual trace that best matches it. In its present form, though, it is still an outreach task rather than a clean experiment: it uses only 10 trials per person, mixes family, polarity and amplitude in the same response set, uses unstructured foils, and does not standardise placement or adaptation history. The strongest scientific news is that I did **not** find an obvious standard thermoception paradigm built around *feel a dynamic thermal waveform, then pick its graph*. What I did find is a fairly coherent neighbouring literature: foundational work on thermal-display design and temperature psychophysics, work on thermal cues in material discrimination, a later strand on **thermal icons** and **thermo-tactile interactions**, and a smaller cross-modal literature on how visual context changes temperature judgments. I would also sharpen one conceptual point straight away: I would **not** lead with “multisensory integration” for the current version. In the present game, the thermal event happens first and the visual traces appear afterwards at response. ## Where the closest literature sits

The closest existing body of work is the **thermal-display / haptics** literature around Lynette Jones and colleagues. The publication trail visible in Jones’s Google Scholar profile includes work on the **psychophysics of temperature perception for interface design**, the **challenge of thermal displays**, and the **contribution of thermal cues to material discrimination and localisation**. Her MIT CV then extends that line into papers on **thermal icons**, **space-time dependencies in thermal perception**, **perceptual interactions in thermo-tactile displays**, **sensory interactions in cutaneous displays**, and the **thermal-tactile simultaneity window**. Taken together, that is very strong evidence that your game sits in a real research programme: the field already studies temperature as a structured information channel, not only as a threshold or comfort variable. citeturn50view0turn46view0

A second nearby literature uses **dynamic thermal transients** to support categorisation of external objects rather than graphs. In a psychophysical study on material recognition via heat transfer, human participants could discriminate materials from the thermal consequences of contact, and the authors showed that changing initial conditions could make different materials thermally ambiguous. That matters for your task because it shows that humans can use transient thermal information as a basis for categorical decision-making, while also showing how easily those judgments can become confusable when dynamics overlap. citeturn33academia1

A third line, mostly in HCI and XR, studies whether temperature can be encoded as **symbolic or patterned feedback** rather than simple hot/cold levels. Recent work has explicitly treated temperature patterns as **thermal icons**, explored their dimensionality, and examined timing constraints in thermo-tactile displays. A separate recent wearable study reached very high identification accuracy, but only across **three** thermal levels, which is informative because it highlights just how ambitious your token space is: your game combines family, polarity and amplitude across a much richer set of stimuli than those simpler identification tasks. citeturn46view0turn26academia0turn0file0?

The cross-modal literature is smaller and more mixed. A preliminary VR study found little effect of visual context on thermal judgments except under very strong cold simulations, whereas a mixed-reality study reported that colour and dynamic graphical effects could systematically shift perceived temperature. Read together, those studies do **not** show that visual context always dominates temperature, but they do show that visual information can alter thermal experience under some conditions. That is directly relevant to your game: participants may be matching a genuine internal thermal representation to a graph, but they may also be partly relying on learned visual metaphors such as “sharp rise = intense event” or “blue/fire graphics = cold/hot.” citeturn27academia2turn60academia0

## Why the paradigm is scientifically valuable

The most compelling scientific value of the game is that it pushes thermoception away from a **single-number** view of temperature. A great deal of classic thermal psychophysics asks about detection thresholds, intensity ratings, discomfort, or material judgments. Your game instead asks whether the system preserves enough information about **temporal form** to support identification of a waveform class. The thermal-display literature strongly suggests that time structure matters for temperature displays, and recent device work shows that dynamic change patterns can strongly shape what people feel, even when mean skin temperature drift is tightly constrained. Scientifically, that makes your task a probe of **temporal coding in thermo-tactile perception**. citeturn50view0turn46view0turn29academia0turn8academia1

The second scientific value is that the paradigm measures **cross-modal externalisation** of a thermal percept. Participants are not merely saying “warm”, “cool”, “strong”, or “weak”; they are selecting a **visualised time-course**. That is interesting because it asks whether a brief thermal event is internally represented in a form that can be mapped onto a spatialised visual trace. That is a more demanding and arguably richer question than simple verbal labelling. It also creates a bridge between basic thermosensation and the wider literature on sensory translation, pseudo-haptics and multimodal interfaces. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn27academia2turn60academia0turn46view0

The third value is analytic. Because the response is a discrete choice among structured alternatives, the task naturally yields **confusion matrices**. Those are scientifically useful because they let you estimate which stimulus dimensions dominate the perceptual space: family, polarity, amplitude, or some combination. That is exactly the sort of logic that has been productive in tactile-pattern and tacton research, and the thermal-display literature’s later focus on **thermal icons** and their **dimensionality** points in the same direction for temperature. Your strongest science may therefore come not from average accuracy, but from the **geometry of errors**. citeturn50view0turn46view0

There is also a mechanistic reason not to assume symmetry. Modern thermosensation does not treat warming and cooling as a single generic axis with opposite signs; innocuous warm and cool sensing involve partially distinct receptors and pathways, and recent mouse work has reinforced the idea of specialised cool pathways. That does **not** by itself prove a human behavioural asymmetry in your exact task, but it gives strong biological justification for testing whether equal-but-opposite thermal traces are psychophysically non-equivalent. citeturn65search5turn61news0

## The clearest questions you can ask

**The temporal-salience question** is, in my view, the best first scientific question: *Which features of a brief thermal waveform make it recognisable?* More concretely, does a fast onset, early peak, or large early derivative make a trace easier to identify than a late-peaking gradual ramp, once overall difficulty is controlled? This is very close to the intuition you already had. It is also strongly motivated by the thermal-display literature’s repeated focus on temporal constraints and by dynamic thermal work showing that change patterns can dominate perception. citeturn46view0turn29academia0turn8academia1

**The threshold-as-question idea** turns your “75% correct” intuition into something publishable. The 75% criterion is **not** the scientific question by itself; it is the *tool* for operationalising one. The actual question would be something like: *What amplitude, or what rise rate, is required for each waveform family to reach criterion identification?* If fast-onset traces reach 75% at smaller |ΔT| than slow-onset traces, then you have a clean result that early temporal change carries more usable perceptual information. In other words, the primary dependent variable becomes **criterion amplitude** or **criterion slope**, not raw score. That is a proper psychophysical question. citeturn50view0turn62academia0

**The cross-modal penalty question** would ask: *How much performance is lost when thermal patterns must be read out through a visual graph rather than compared within the thermal modality?* The clean comparison would be the current **feel → schematic** task versus a **feel → replayed thermal candidate** task or a **thermal target → draw/adjust a curve** task. If performance drops selectively in the graph condition, then the cross-modal translation is doing real cognitive work. If performance is similar across tasks, then the visual schematic may genuinely capture the internal structure of the percept. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn27academia2turn60academia0

**The warm–cool asymmetry question** would ask: *Are matched warming and cooling envelopes equally identifiable?* That is worth testing because the biology does not compel mirror-image percepts, and because HCI work on thermal displays and material discrimination repeatedly treats cooling and warming as behaviourally meaningful signals rather than interchangeable opposites. I would still treat this as a hypothesis to test rather than an assumption to build into the design. citeturn50view0turn33academia1turn65search5turn61news0

**The representational-geometry question** would ask: *When people are wrong, what are they wrong about?* Do they preserve polarity but confuse family? Preserve family but miss amplitude? Collapse everything onto a “fast event versus gradual event” dimension? This is where your game becomes genuinely interesting, because confusions can tell you whether the thermal system is coding something closer to **signed intensity**, **temporal envelope**, or **symbolic category**. The later work on thermal icons and their dimensionality is exactly the literature that makes this framing legible to reviewers. citeturn46view0turn50view0

## How to convert the game into publishable psychophysics

The first redesign move is to separate **outreach mode** from **experiment mode**. The current 10-trial public version is valuable as a demonstration and an exploratory pilot, but it should not be over-interpreted. For science, you need repeated within-subject sampling, a fixed body site, a recorded inter-trial recovery rule, and a foil strategy that tests specific confusions rather than drawing arbitrary alternatives from the whole pool. The brief itself already identifies these limitations very clearly. fileciteturn0file0

The second move is to add a **calibration stage** before identification. Do not start with the full 6-AFC and then ask what the score means. First, estimate a participant-specific criterion for a reduced discrimination problem. That can be done with a staircase or another adaptive method on amplitude, rise rate, or time-to-peak, depending on which scientific question you want. Only after that should you run the richer identification block. This follows the standard psychophysical logic that criterion performance is part of measurement, not the final theoretical claim. citeturn50view0turn62academia0

The third move is to include at least one **control condition** that tells you whether an “easy” trace is easy on the skin, easy on the screen, or both. The simplest control is visual-only: present the visual traces in a matching task with no thermal stimulus and estimate which schematics are visually discriminable. If the same traces that are easiest visually are also easiest thermally, your main effect may partly reflect graph readability. That matters especially because the current brief already acknowledges that the response options are idealised theoretical traces and that visual similarity may not match perceptual similarity. fileciteturn0file0

The fourth move is to analyse **measured** rather than merely commanded stimulus features. Your platform already records optional measured thermal traces and calibration plots, which is a major strength. Use that. If you want to claim that “fast onset” matters, the independent variable should be the **realised** onset slope, time-to-peak, return slope, and area-under-the-curve at the skin–thermode interface, not only the command values in software. This is especially important for your nominally very fast segments, because thermal mass and contact mechanics can compress different commands into more similar realised transients. fileciteturn0file0

The fifth move is to collect richer dependent variables. Accuracy alone will undersell the paradigm. You should also record **reaction time**, **confidence**, and the full **confusion matrix**. Those measures let you distinguish “easy because salient” from “easy because confidently categorised”, and they let you test whether criterion-matched stimuli still differ in decision speed or certainty. In thermal-display work, temporal and spatial disparities are already recognised as important design issues, so RT and confidence are not optional extras here; they help tell you which part of the perceptual pipeline is struggling. citeturn46view0turn50view0

Analytically, I would model trial-level choices with a hierarchical multinomial or mixed-effects framework using measured features such as signed peak change, onset slope, offset slope, and time-to-peak as predictors, with participant as a random factor. Then I would derive feature-level confusion summaries: family confusions, polarity confusions, amplitude confusions, and mutual information carried by each dimension. That approach is much more scientific than ranking stimuli by the raw number of correct identifications in an opportunistic sample. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn50view0turn62academia0

## The strongest first paper

If the goal is a first peer-reviewable paper rather than a sprawling platform paper, I would recommend this as the central question:

*Do humans identify the temporal form of brief cutaneous thermal waveforms, and which waveform features reach criterion performance with the smallest physical temperature excursion?* citeturn46view0turn50view0turn29academia0

That question lets you use your 75% idea cleanly. In practice, I would run it in two stages. First, use adaptive calibration to estimate, for each participant and waveform family, the amplitude needed to reach roughly 75% performance in a reduced family-identification task. Second, run a fully crossed identification block near that participant-specific criterion with structured foils designed to isolate family, polarity and amplitude confusions. The main outcome is then **criterion amplitude by family**; the secondary outcomes are **confusion structure, RT and confidence**. citeturn50view0turn62academia0turn46view0

This design gives you a crisp theoretical interpretation. If fast-onset or early-peaking traces reach criterion with smaller |ΔT|, then early thermal transients are more perceptually diagnostic than slower ones. If all families require similar amplitudes but are confused differently, then the temporal information is present but categorically organised in a more complex way. If differences disappear after a visual-only control, then the effect is largely schematic/readout-driven rather than thermo-perceptual. And if a later **feel → thermal replay** version outperforms **feel → graph**, then you have isolated a bona fide cross-modal translation cost. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn27academia2turn60academia0turn33academia1turn46view0
